If you're searching for how to pass the ARDMS SPI exam on your first try, you're probably juggling clinicals, classes, work, or all three, and you don't have time to waste on the wrong study plan. Good news: the Sonography Principles and Instrumentation (SPI) exam is very passable when you know what's on it, practice the way the exam actually tests you, and walk in with a pacing plan. This guide covers all three.

## SPI exam basics: what you're signing up for

Before you build a plan, get the facts straight:

- **Who runs it:** The SPI is an ARDMS exam, administered by Inteleos/ARDMS. You take it at a **Pearson VUE** test center.
- **Format:** About **110 multiple-choice questions** in **2 hours**. That time includes a short survey, so plan on slightly less than the full 120 minutes for the questions themselves.
- **Scoring:** Scores fall on a **300–700 scale**, and **555 is passing**.
- **Why it matters:** The SPI is required, along with a specialty exam, for the **RDMS, RDCS, RVT and RMSKS** credentials. Passing it is the first major step toward your credential no matter which specialty you choose.

### What the current content outline emphasizes

ARDMS updated the SPI content outline in **September 2023**. Under the current outline, **Doppler concepts and image optimization are the largest areas**, and together they make up a majority of the exam. That matters for how you divide your time: if you spend most of your study hours on the history of ultrasound or memorizing a handful of formulas, you're under-preparing for the bulk of the test.

We're deliberately not listing exact percentages here. Download the official SPI content outline from [ardms.org](https://www.ardms.org) and use it as your checklist. It's the only source that's guaranteed to be current.

## How long should you study? An 8–12 week plan

Most students do well with **8 to 12 weeks** of consistent study. Here's a 10-week plan you can stretch or compress. Notice how much of it goes to Doppler and image optimization.

| Weeks | Focus | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Baseline + physics foundations | Take a timed practice set to find your starting point. Review sound waves, frequency, wavelength, propagation speed, attenuation. |
| 2 | Transducers and beam formation | Piezoelectric principles, focusing, resolution (axial, lateral, elevational). |
| 3–4 | Image optimization | Gain, TGC, depth, focus, dynamic range, frame rate, harmonics, compounding, artifacts and how to fix them. |
| 5–7 | Doppler concepts and hemodynamics | Doppler equation and angle, spectral vs. color vs. power Doppler, aliasing and Nyquist, PRF, wall filters, flow patterns, spectral waveform interpretation. |
| 8 | Safety, bioeffects, patient care, QA | ALARA, thermal and mechanical indices, infection control, quality assurance. |
| 9 | Full-length mock exams | Two timed mocks, with full review of every miss. |
| 10 | Targeted review | Drill your weakest categories, take a final mock, rest the day before. |

If you only have 8 weeks, combine weeks 1–2 and trim week 8. If you have 12, add a second pass through image optimization and Doppler, since that's where the points are.

## The #1 mistake: studying passively

The most common reason well-prepared students fall short is that they spent their time **re-reading** notes and textbooks instead of **retrieving** information. Reading feels productive because the material looks familiar. But the SPI doesn't ask whether something looks familiar; it asks you to apply it to a scenario you haven't seen before.

Active recall means forcing your brain to pull the answer out before you see it:

- **Answer questions before reviewing a chapter**, not just after. Getting things wrong first makes the explanation stick.
- **Use flashcards honestly.** Say the answer out loud before you flip. If you hesitated, mark it as missed.
- **Explain it in your own words.** If you can't explain why increasing PRF reduces aliasing to a classmate, you don't own the concept yet.
- **Review every explanation, including the ones you got right.** A lucky guess is still a gap.

A practical rule: for every hour of reading, spend at least an hour answering questions and reviewing explanations.

## How many practice questions should you do?

Aim for **500–700+ practice questions** before exam day, plus at least two or three **full-length timed mock exams**. Volume matters, but only if you review each question. Three hundred questions reviewed carefully beats a thousand clicked through.

Here's how to use them:

1. **Early on, study by category.** Work through one topic at a time so you build understanding before speed.
2. **Mid-plan, mix topics.** The real exam doesn't group questions by chapter, so your practice shouldn't either.
3. **Late in your plan, go full-length and timed.** You need to know what minute 90 feels like.
4. **Track weak categories.** Your accuracy by topic tells you where the next week of study should go.

## Exam-day strategy

You've done the work. Now protect it.

### Before you start

- **Arrive about 30 minutes early** (check your confirmation email for specifics).
- Bring **valid, unexpired government ID** with a name that **matches your registration** exactly.
- Your personal items go in a **locker**. You'll be given an **erasable note board** to use during the exam.

### Pacing

With about 110 questions and a little under 2 hours once the survey is done, budget roughly **one minute per question**. Do a quick check-in around the halfway mark: you want to be near question 55 at about the one-hour point. If a question is eating your time, pick your best answer, **flag it**, and move on.

### Use your note board

As soon as the exam starts, jot down anything you're nervous about forgetting: the Doppler equation, the relationships between PRF, depth and frame rate, and which settings affect which resolution. Then you can stop holding them in your head.

### Should you change answers?

You've probably heard "always go with your first instinct." The evidence doesn't really support that. Research on answer changing generally finds that changed answers go from **wrong to right more often than right to wrong**. The useful rule is:

- **Change an answer when you have a specific reason**, like you misread the question, or a later question reminded you of a fact.
- **Don't change an answer out of anxiety** or just because it "feels" wrong on a second look.

### After the exam

You'll receive a **score report** after the exam, and it will also be available in your ARDMS account. If it doesn't go your way, ARDMS sets a waiting period between attempts, so check the current retake policy on ardms.org and use that time to target your weak areas.
