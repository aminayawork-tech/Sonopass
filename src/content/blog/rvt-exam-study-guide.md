The RVT asks you to do two things at once: understand the physics of the sound beam and read what the blood is telling you. One minute you're calculating a velocity ratio across a femoral stenosis. The next you're deciding whether a reversed vertebral waveform means subclavian steal. This RVT exam study guide covers how the credential works, what the Vascular Technology (VT) exam covers, the high-yield numbers worth memorizing (and where they vary), and an 8-week plan you can start this week.

## RVT basics: two exams, one credential

The Registered Vascular Technologist (RVT) credential from ARDMS takes **two passing scores**:

- **SPI (Sonography Principles and Instrumentation):** about **110 multiple-choice questions** in **2 hours**, which includes a short survey.
- **VT (Vascular Technology):** the specialty exam. Like most ARDMS specialty exams, it runs about **3 hours**.

Both are scored on a **300–700 scale**, and **555 is passing**. You take them at **Pearson VUE** test centers. Prerequisites depend on your education and clinical background, so confirm your pathway on ardms.org before you apply.

If you haven't taken the SPI yet, start with [how to pass the ARDMS SPI exam on your first try](/blog/how-to-pass-ardms-spi-exam-first-try). A lot of SPI Doppler knowledge carries straight into the VT exam.

## What the VT exam covers

ARDMS publishes the official VT content outline, with the domains and their weights. **Check the official VT content outline on ardms.org** for the current question count and percentages. It's the only source that's guaranteed to be up to date, and it's the checklist your study plan should follow.

In practice, the material breaks down into these topic areas:

### Hemodynamics

This is the foundation for everything else.

- **Flow = pressure difference / resistance.** Flow needs a pressure gradient.
- **Poiseuille's law:** resistance is directly related to vessel length and blood viscosity, and inversely related to the **radius to the fourth power**. Halve the radius and resistance goes up 16-fold.
- **Continuity equation:** flow = area × velocity. When the lumen narrows, velocity rises.
- **Bernoulli principle:** where velocity rises (in a stenosis), pressure falls. Energy is lost as flow leaves the stenosis.
- **Reynolds number:** turbulence becomes likely above a Reynolds number of about 2,000.
- **Critical stenosis:** a 50% diameter reduction is about a 75% area reduction. That's around the point where flow and pressure begin to drop measurably at rest.
- **Waveform resistance:** high-resistance beds (resting limbs, external carotid) show sharp, multiphasic waveforms. Low-resistance beds (brain, kidneys, liver) show continuous forward flow in diastole.

### Cerebrovascular

Extracranial carotid and vertebral duplex, plus transcranial Doppler. Know how to tell the **ICA (low resistance)** from the **ECA (high resistance, branches, responds to the temporal tap)**. Know the vertebral flow patterns that point to subclavian steal, from a mid-systolic dip all the way to fully reversed flow.

### Peripheral arterial

Lower and upper extremity duplex, bypass graft surveillance and post-intervention scans. A normal resting extremity artery is typically multiphasic (often triphasic). Distal to a significant obstruction, waveforms become monophasic with a delayed upstroke. Many labs use a **peak systolic velocity ratio of about 2 or higher** across a segment to suggest a 50% or greater stenosis, but criteria vary by lab.

### Peripheral venous

DVT evaluation, venous insufficiency and vein mapping. **Compressibility is the primary criterion for DVT**: a normal vein collapses completely with transducer pressure. Supporting signs include spontaneous and phasic flow, and augmentation with distal compression. Know how acute and chronic thrombus differ in appearance.

### Abdominal and visceral vascular

Aorta and iliacs, renal arteries, mesenteric arteries, and the hepatoportal system. Know normal waveforms: the **renal arteries are low resistance**, and the **fasting SMA is high resistance** and becomes low resistance after a meal.

### Physiologic (indirect) testing

Ankle-brachial index (ABI), segmental pressures, pulse volume recordings (PVR), toe pressures and exercise testing. These test **function** rather than anatomy, and exam questions often ask you to put the pieces together: where is the disease, given these pressures and waveforms?

### Patient care, safety and documentation

Infection control, patient identification, positioning and communication, plus documentation and quality practices. These questions are straightforward points if you review them.

## High-yield values (and where they vary)

Memorize these, but know that **many diagnostic criteria vary by lab and by consensus document**. On the exam, read the question for any criteria it gives you. In the lab, follow your lab's protocol.

### Ankle-brachial index

**ABI = higher ankle pressure (DP or PT) on that leg / higher of the two brachial pressures.**

Commonly used interpretation (per AHA guidance):

| ABI | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| > 1.40 | Noncompressible (calcified) vessels; result unreliable |
| 1.00–1.40 | Normal |
| 0.91–0.99 | Borderline |
| ≤ 0.90 | Abnormal, consistent with peripheral arterial disease |

Severity cutoffs below 0.90 (mild, moderate, severe) differ between sources. When vessels are noncompressible, toe pressures and the toe-brachial index are used instead.

### Internal carotid stenosis

The **2003 Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound (SRU) consensus criteria** are widely taught:

| ICA stenosis | ICA PSV | ICA/CCA PSV ratio | ICA EDV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | < 125 cm/s, no plaque | < 2.0 | < 40 cm/s |
| < 50% | < 125 cm/s | < 2.0 | < 40 cm/s |
| 50–69% | 125–230 cm/s | 2.0–4.0 | 40–100 cm/s |
| ≥ 70% (not near occlusion) | > 230 cm/s | > 4.0 | > 100 cm/s |
| Near occlusion | High, low or undetectable | Variable | Variable |
| Total occlusion | No flow | Not applicable | Not applicable |

Plenty of labs use their own validated criteria instead, so treat this table as the common reference, not the only answer.

### Venous reflux

Under criteria based on Labropoulos and colleagues and adopted by Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines, reflux is usually defined as reversed flow lasting:

- **More than 0.5 seconds** in superficial truncal veins, tibial veins, the deep femoral vein and perforators.
- **More than 1 second** in the common femoral, femoral and popliteal veins.

### Renal and mesenteric

Values such as a renal-to-aortic ratio above 3.5, or SMA and celiac peak velocity thresholds, appear in many references. They vary more than most criteria, and they depend on technique and the study they came from. Learn the concept (a jump in velocity at the origin, with poststenotic turbulence and a damped downstream waveform), then learn the specific numbers your textbook and review sources use.

## An 8-week RVT study plan

This plan assumes you've already passed the SPI or are studying for it separately, and that you can study about an hour a day on weekdays with a longer session on weekends.

| Week | Focus | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Baseline + hemodynamics | Timed mixed set to find your starting point. Poiseuille, Bernoulli, continuity, resistance and waveform shapes. |
| 2 | Cerebrovascular | Carotid and vertebral anatomy, ICA vs. ECA, stenosis criteria, subclavian steal, TCD basics. |
| 3 | Peripheral arterial | Lower and upper extremity anatomy, waveform changes, velocity ratios, grafts and stents. |
| 4 | Physiologic testing | ABI, segmental pressures, PVR, toe pressures, exercise testing. Practice localizing disease from the numbers. |
| 5 | Peripheral venous | DVT criteria, acute vs. chronic thrombus, reflux testing, vein mapping. |
| 6 | Abdominal and visceral | Aorta and iliacs, renal, mesenteric, hepatoportal. Patient care and documentation review. |
| 7 | Full-length mocks | Two timed mocks, three hours each. Review every miss and every lucky guess. |
| 8 | Targeted review | Drill your two weakest categories. Final mock early in the week, then light review and rest. |

Each week, split your time: about **one-third learning, two-thirds answering questions and reviewing explanations**. Reading feels productive, but retrieving answers is what builds exam-day recall.
