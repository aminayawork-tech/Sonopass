Search for the best sonography board exam prep course and you'll find a long list of options: thick textbooks, lecture series, online question banks, weekend review seminars and study apps. They're not all trying to do the same job, which is why comparing them head-to-head can be confusing. This guide breaks prep resources down by **type**, explains what each one is good at, and helps you choose a primary resource that fits how you actually study.

## First, know what you're preparing for

ARDMS registry exams are administered by Inteleos/ARDMS and taken at Pearson VUE test centers. Most candidates start with the **SPI** (about 110 questions in 2 hours), which is required alongside a specialty exam for the RDMS, RDCS, RVT and RMSKS credentials. Specialty exams such as **Abdomen** typically run about 3 hours; the Abdomen exam has about 165 questions, including hotspot (image) questions. Scores are reported on a 300–700 scale, and 555 passes.

That tells you what good prep has to deliver: solid understanding of the content, lots of practice applying it to exam-style questions, and the stamina and pacing to do it under time pressure.

## The main types of sonography board prep

| Resource type | Best for | Practice questions | Timed full-length mocks | Study on the go | Typical pacing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Textbooks and lecture series | Deep explanations of concepts | Varies; often chapter-end questions | Varies | Limited | Self-paced, reading-heavy |
| Online question banks | Exam-style practice and repetition | Core feature | Often included | Usually browser-based | Self-paced |
| Live review seminars | Structured, instructor-led review | Varies | Varies | No (scheduled sessions) | Fixed schedule |
| All-in-one study apps | Daily practice, review and tracking in one place | Core feature | Often included | Yes, built for phones | Self-paced, short sessions |

The details vary a lot from product to product within each type, so always check what a specific resource includes before you buy.

## Textbooks and lecture series

Textbooks and recorded or live lecture series are where many students first learn sonography physics and anatomy. **Pegasus Lectures**, for example, is well known for its ultrasound physics textbooks and lectures. Check their site for current offerings.

**Strengths:**
- Thorough explanations that build concepts from the ground up.
- Useful as a reference when a topic just isn't clicking.

**Trade-offs:**
- Reading and watching are mostly passive. You need to add active practice on top.
- Harder to fit into five spare minutes between patients or classes.

## Online question banks

Question banks focus on what the exam actually asks you to do: answer questions. **Ultrasound Registry Review**, for example, is well known for online registry review questions. Check their site for current offerings.

**Strengths:**
- Builds the skill of applying knowledge to exam-style scenarios.
- Explanations turn missed questions into learning.

**Trade-offs:**
- Quality and depth of explanations vary by provider.
- Some are designed mainly for desktop use, which matters if you mostly study on your phone.

## Live review seminars

Review seminars pack a lot of content into a scheduled session, often a day or a weekend, led by an instructor.

**Strengths:**
- Structure and accountability, plus the chance to ask questions in real time.
- Good for a final push if you like learning in a group.

**Trade-offs:**
- Fixed dates and times can be hard to fit around clinicals and work.
- A seminar is a snapshot. You still need ongoing practice before and after.

## All-in-one study apps

All-in-one apps combine practice questions, explanations, mock exams, review tools and progress tracking in one place, built for the way most students actually study: in short sessions, on their phones, between everything else.

**Strengths:**
- Active practice every day, even when you only have a few minutes.
- Progress tracking that points you to your weak areas automatically.
- Timed mocks to rehearse exam conditions.

**Trade-offs:**
- If you're missing foundational understanding of a topic, you may want a textbook alongside the app (more on that below).

## When a textbook still helps

We'll be honest: an app isn't a replacement for every kind of learning. A textbook or lecture series can be worth adding if:

- You're **new to a subject** and need it explained from scratch, such as SPI physics before you've had a physics course.
- You keep missing questions on the **same concept** even after reading the explanations, and you need a longer, step-by-step walkthrough.
- Your program already assigns a text and you want to stay aligned with your coursework.

In those cases, use the textbook to build understanding and an app to turn that understanding into exam-ready recall.

## A simple decision framework

- **Use an all-in-one app as your primary resource if** you study in short bursts, want daily practice and timed mocks, and want your weak areas tracked for you.
- **Add a textbook or lecture series if** you need foundational explanations for a topic you've never formally learned.
- **Add a live review seminar if** you learn best in a group, need outside structure, and the schedule fits your life.
- **Use a standalone question bank if** you already have your own study system and only want extra questions.

Whatever you choose, make sure your plan includes three things: **active practice**, **review of every explanation**, and **at least two or three timed full-length mock exams** before test day. And always check the official content outline for your exam on [ardms.org](https://www.ardms.org) so you know what to prioritize.
